The Sun

Our star — the engine of light, heat, and gravity for the entire Solar System.

G-type main-sequence star

Drag to rotate · Scroll to zoom

At a Glance

  • Diameter1,392,700 km
  • Mass1.989 × 10³⁰ kg (99.86% of Solar System)
  • Surface gravity274 m/s² (28× Earth)
  • Rotation (equator)~25.4 Earth days
  • Age~4.6 billion years
  • Photosphere temp.~5,500 °C
  • Core temp.~15 million °C
  • Composition~73% H, ~25% He
  • Distance from Earth~150 million km (1 AU)

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star at the center of our Solar System. Through nuclear fusion in its core, it converts hydrogen into helium, releasing the energy that powers weather, climate, photosynthesis, and virtually all life on Earth. Its gravity binds the planets, dwarf planets, asteroids, and comets into stable orbits that have persisted for billions of years.

Key Facts

  • Light from the Sun reaches Earth in about 8 minutes and 20 seconds.
  • The Sun fuses roughly 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium every second.
  • It is about 109 times wider than Earth and could fit ~1.3 million Earths inside its volume.
  • Solar activity follows an approximately 11-year cycle of sunspots, flares, and coronal mass ejections.
  • The visible surface (photosphere) is only the outer shell; most of the Sun is hot plasma.
  • During a total solar eclipse, the Moon blocks the photosphere and reveals the faint, million-degree corona.
  • The Sun will eventually exhaust its core hydrogen, expand into a red giant, and later become a white dwarf.
  • It has been shining for about 4.6 billion years and has roughly 5 billion years of stable fusion left.
  • Solar wind — a stream of charged particles — extends far beyond Pluto, shaping the heliosphere.
  • Without the Sun's steady energy output, Earth's oceans would freeze and life as we know it could not exist.

Structure & Discovery

  • Humans have worshipped and studied the Sun since prehistory; ancient cultures tracked its seasonal motion.
  • Modern heliophysics uses space telescopes like SDO and Parker Solar Probe to study the Sun up close.
  • The core is where fusion occurs; energy then radiates outward through the radiative and convective zones.
