Mars

The Red Planet — dusty deserts, ancient rivers, and two tiny moons.

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and the most explored world beyond Earth. Its iron-rich dust gives the surface a distinctive red hue. Orbital and rover missions have found evidence that liquid water once flowed across Mars, making it a prime target in the search for past microbial life and a future destination for human exploration.

Key Facts

  • Olympus Mons is the tallest volcano in the Solar System — about 22 km high and 600 km wide.
  • Valles Marineris is a canyon system stretching over 4,000 km — far longer and deeper than the Grand Canyon.
  • Mars has polar ice caps made of water ice and frozen carbon dioxide that grow and shrink with the seasons.
  • Global dust storms can engulf the entire planet for weeks, blocking sunlight from solar-powered rovers.
  • River valleys, lake beds, and minerals that form in water all point to a wetter past ~3 billion years ago.
  • The thin atmosphere cannot support liquid water on the surface today, but briny water may exist briefly.
  • NASA's Perseverance rover is collecting samples for possible return to Earth in the 2030s.
  • Mars has seasons like Earth because of a similar axial tilt (25.2°), but each season lasts nearly twice as long.
  • Evidence suggests Mars lost most of its atmosphere and magnetic field billions of years ago.
  • Multiple nations and private companies plan crewed missions to Mars within the coming decades.

Moons

Phobos

Diameter: 22.4 km Orbit period: 7.65 hours Distance: 9,376 km
  • Phobos orbits Mars faster than the planet rotates — it rises in the west and sets in the east.
  • It is heavily cratered, with the giant Stickney crater dominating one face.
  • Phobos is spiraling inward and will either crash into Mars or break apart within ~50 million years.
  • Its low density suggests it may be a rubble pile or captured asteroid.

Deimos

Diameter: 12.4 km Orbit period: 30.3 hours Distance: 23,460 km
  • Deimos is the smaller and outer of Mars's two moons, with a smoother surface than Phobos.
  • From Mars, it appears star-like because it is too small to show a visible disk.
  • Like Phobos, Deimos is irregularly shaped and likely a captured asteroid from the asteroid belt.
  • Its orbit is slowly expanding as tidal forces gradually push it away from Mars.
