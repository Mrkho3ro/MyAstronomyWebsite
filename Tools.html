Telescope Types

1. Dobsonian Reflectors

Simple alt-azimuth mounts paired with large mirrors — maximum aperture per dollar for sweeping the deep sky.

2. Newtonian Reflectors

Isaac Newton's mirror design remains the workhorse of amateur astronomy on equatorial and Dobsonian mounts alike.

3. Refracting Telescopes

Lens-based tubes deliver crisp, high-contrast views of the Moon and planets in a portable package.

4. Schmidt-Cassegrain

Mirrors and a correcting lens plate fold light into a compact tube — ideal for GoTo tracking and long focal lengths.

5. Maksutov-Cassegrain

Thick meniscus corrector lenses produce rugged, closed-tube scopes prized for lunar and planetary detail.

6. Radio Telescopes

Parabolic dishes and interferometers tune into hydrogen, pulsars, and signals invisible to the human eye.

History of the Telescope

Four centuries of glass, mirrors, and ingenuity — from a Dutch spectacle-maker's workshop to observatories orbiting above Earth's turbulent atmosphere.

1608

1608 — The First Light-Gatherers

In the autumn of 1608, the Dutch spectacle-maker Hans Lipperhey applied for a patent on an instrument that magnified distant objects using convex and concave lenses. Word spread quickly across Europe. By the following year, Galileo Galilei had built his own improved version and turned it toward the night sky — revealing the craters of the Moon, the phases of Venus, and four moons orbiting Jupiter.

1668

Newton's Mirror

Early refractors suffered from chromatic aberration: different colors of light focused at slightly different points, blurring fine detail. In 1668, Isaac Newton presented a radical alternative — a telescope that gathered light with a curved mirror instead of a lens. The Newtonian reflector produced images free of false color and remains popular today.

Replica of Newton's 1668 reflector — Science Museum UK / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)

1780s

1780s — Herschel and the Giant Mirrors

William Herschel pushed reflecting telescopes to unprecedented scale, grinding mirrors by hand. With a 48-inch reflector he discovered Uranus in 1781 and cataloged thousands of nebulae and double stars, fundamentally expanding humanity's map of the deep sky.

1758

The Achromatic Breakthrough

John Dollond solved the refractor's color problem by pairing crown-glass and flint-glass lenses. Achromatic refractors became the standard for precision planetary observation throughout the nineteenth century and remain prized for sharp, high-contrast views.

Modern Mounts and the Dobsonian Revolution

Equatorial mounts and GoTo systems automated star tracking. John Dobson's rocker-box design paired with large Newtonian mirrors put deep-sky observing within reach of ordinary enthusiasts. Schmidt-Cassegrain and Maksutov designs folded long focal lengths into portable tubes.

Modern Dobsonian reflector — Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

1990 — Today

Space Telescopes — Hubble and Beyond

Launched in 1990, the Hubble Space Telescope orbits above Earth's atmosphere, delivering sharp images across ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared wavelengths. The James Webb Space Telescope, operating primarily in the infrared since 2022, peers through dust to study the earliest galaxies.

James Webb Space Telescope primary mirror — NASA / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

1932

1932 — Listening to the Sky

Karl Jansky detected a persistent hiss from the Milky Way while investigating radio static. His discovery opened an entirely new window on the universe — cold hydrogen clouds, spinning pulsars, and the faint afterglow of the Big Bang. Arrays like the VLA and FAST achieve resolutions rivaling optical telescopes.
